Part B: Debits and Credits

The Accounting Cycle During the Period — recording, posting, and proving the books with a trial balance

Debit Just Means "Left"

In Part A we talked about accounts increasing and decreasing. Accountants have a shorthand for this: debit simply means the left side of an account, and credit simply means the right side. Neither word inherently means "increase" or "decrease" — what matters is which side of the accounting equation the account sits on.

Left Side

Debit

Assets increase with a debit and decrease with a credit.

Right Side

Credit

Liabilities and Stockholders’ Equity increase with a credit and decrease with a debit.

Common Mistake

Debit does not always mean "increase," and credit does not always mean "decrease." That's only true for assets. Liabilities and stockholders' equity work the opposite way — they increase with a credit.

Revenues, Expenses & Dividends

Since revenues, expenses, and dividends all roll up into retained earnings (a stockholders' equity account), their debit/credit rules follow from how they affect retained earnings:

ComponentEffect on Retained EarningsIncreases With
RevenuesIncreaseCredit
ExpensesDecreaseDebit
DividendsDecreaseDebit

Key Point — A Memory Aid

D-E-A (Dividends, Expenses, Assets) increase with a debit. L-O-R (Liabilities, Owners’ Equity, Revenues) increase with a credit. Each decreases with the opposite entry.

Concept Check 2-3

(a) Debit means left side; credit means right side. Whether that side represents an increase or a decrease depends entirely on which type of account it is.

Recording Journal Entries

A journal entry is the formal record of a transaction's debits and credits. By convention:

  • The debit account is listed first, flush left.
  • The credit account is listed second, indented to the right.
  • Total debits must equal total credits — every single time.

Here's transaction 1 from Part A — Nantahala Paddling Academy issuing common stock for $250,000 — written as a journal entry:

DateAccountDebitCredit
Dec 1Cash250,000
Common Stock250,000

Common Mistake

Forgetting to indent the credit account is one of the most common journal-entry slip-ups. Both the credit account's name and its dollar amount get indented — it should be visually obvious which account was debited and which was credited.

Posting to the General Ledger

Posting is the process of moving each journal entry's debits and credits into individual accounts. A T-account is a simplified ledger account: the title on top, debits on the left, credits on the right.

Concept Check 2-4

Which of the following provides a chronological record of all transactions affecting a company?

  1. The general ledger
  2. The journal
  3. The trial balance
  4. The income statement
(b) The journal records transactions in the order they happen. The general ledger accumulates account balances; the trial balance summarizes those balances; the income statement reports profitability.

  1. Cash
  2. Rent Expense
  3. Prepaid Rent
  4. Rental Income
(c) Prepaid Rent is an asset, so it increases with a debit. (Cash is credited for the same amount.)

The Trial Balance

A trial balance is a list of every account and its balance at a point in time, proving that total debits equal total credits. It's an internal check, never published to outside parties, and there's no required order — though most companies list assets, liabilities, equity, dividends, revenues, then expenses, since that order makes the financial statements easier to prepare afterward.

Common Mistake

A trial balance that balances doesn't guarantee every number is correct. If Cash and Service Revenue were each overstated by $1,000, the trial balance would still balance perfectly — the errors offset each other. Balancing proves debits equal credits; it doesn't prove the numbers themselves are right.
