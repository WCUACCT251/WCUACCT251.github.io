Part A

Adjusting the accounts

Before any financial statement is ready, the books need one last update. Here’s why, and how it’s done.

Where we are in the accounting cycle

So far, we’ve recorded external transactions as they happened during the period. But the last day of the period brings three more jobs, and the books aren’t finished until all three are done.

  1. Adjust. Bring every account up to date for activity that hasn’t been recorded yet. This finishes the measurement process.
  2. Report. Use the updated balances to prepare the financial statements. This is the reporting process.
  3. Close. Reset the temporary accounts to zero so the next period starts fresh. This is the closing process.

These notes cover the first job. Part B covers the second, and closing comes after that.

Accrual-basis accounting: record it when it happens

Every transaction we’ve recorded so far has followed accrual-basis accounting. The idea is to capture economic events when they actually occur, not when money happens to change hands, so decision makers get timely and accurate information.

Under the accrual basis, we record:

  • Assetswhen the company acquires the resource.
  • Liabilitieswhen the company takes on the obligation.
  • Revenueswhen the company delivers goods or services to customers.
  • Expenseswhen the company uses up a cost to run the business.

A rafting outfitter that guides a trip in December reports that revenue in December, even if the customer pays in January. The fuel, guide wages, and gear used on that trip belong in December too, alongside the revenue they helped create. Some costs, like office rent or advertising, don’t tie neatly to one specific sale, so we simply record them in the period they occur.

Key point

Accrual-basis accounting records events that affect assets, liabilities, revenues, and expenses at the time those events occur.

Accrual basis versus cash basis

The easiest way to understand the accrual basis is to compare it with the alternative. Under cash-basis accounting, a transaction gets recorded only when cash comes in or goes out. No cash, no entry.

Let’s return to Nantahala Paddling Academy, the whitewater outfitter we followed through its first December. Here’s how three of its revenue transactions look under each method.

Same totals, different timing

Over the long run, both methods end up recording the same total revenue and expense. The $9,000 advance will become revenue under both methods eventually: under the accrual basis once the trips happen, under the cash basis the day the money arrived. The full $48,000 of rent will be expensed under both methods once the year is up. The only difference is when.

Sometimes there’s no difference at all. When customers pay on the spot (Dec 10) or the company pays wages for work done that same month (Dec 27), the cash flow and the revenue or expense land in the same period under either method.

Cash-basis accounting feels natural because it’s how most of us track a checking account. But it isn’t part of generally accepted accounting principles (GAAP), so major companies use the accrual basis for their financial statements.

Common mistake

It’s tempting to think revenue can’t be recorded until cash shows up, or that an expense waits until the bill is paid. Under the accrual basis, revenue follows delivery and expenses follow use, whenever the cash happens to move.

Key point

The difference between the two methods is timing. Accrual basis: revenue when goods or services are delivered, expenses when costs are used. Cash basis: revenue when cash is received, expenses when cash is paid. Most major companies can’t use the cash basis for financial reporting.

Adjusting entries: the four types

The accrual basis creates gaps between when cash moves and when revenue or expense is recognized. Those gaps live on the balance sheet as assets and liabilities, and by the end of the period some of them are out of date.

Adjusting entries fix that. They’re recorded on the last day of the period to update assets and liabilities, along with the revenues and expenses tied to them, for activity that happened but hasn’t been recorded. Rent expires a little every day, but nobody records rent expense every day. We wait until period-end and make one entry for the whole period.

Every adjustment fits one of four types, grouped by whether cash moved before or after the revenue or expense.

Prepayments: cash moves first, revenue or expense comes later

Prepaid expense

The company pays in advance for something it will use later, like rent, supplies, insurance, or equipment. It’s recorded as an asset.

Adjustment: record the expense, reduce the asset.

Deferred revenue

Customers pay in advance for goods or services they’ll receive later, like season passes, memberships, or gift cards. It’s recorded as a liability.

Adjustment: reduce the liability, record the revenue.

Accruals: revenue or expense comes first, cash moves later

Accrued expense

The company has used a cost this period, like wages, utilities, interest, or taxes, but won’t pay until next period.

Adjustment: record the expense, record a liability (payable).

Accrued revenue

The company has delivered goods or services, or earned interest, but hasn’t collected or even billed yet.

Adjustment: record an asset (receivable), record the revenue.

Key point

Adjusting entries record changes in assets and liabilities, and their related revenues and expenses, that happened during the period but haven’t been recorded by period-end. Prepayments need an adjustment when cash came first; accruals need one when cash will come later.

Nantahala’s December 31 adjustments

All ten of Nantahala’s December transactions are already in the books. On December 31, a review turns up eight items that happened during the month but haven’t been recorded. Each card shows the adjusting entry and what it does to the accounts.

Prepaid expenses

a

Adjustment a: Boathouse rent expires

Dec 31

On Dec 3, Nantahala prepaid a year of boathouse rent: $48,000, or $4,000 a month. One month of that rent has now been used.

Adjusting entry, December 31

Rent expense$4,000none

Common mistake

The Dec 3 cash payment is not the adjustment. That entry recorded the asset. The adjustment comes later and records the portion that has been used up.

b

Adjustment b: Supplies get used

Dec 31

Prepaid expense

Nantahala bought $18,000 of paddles, helmets, and life vests on Dec 5. Nobody records supplies as they’re used, so on Dec 31 the staff counts what’s left: $11,000.

Adjusting entry, December 31
AccountDebitCredit
Supplies expense$7,000none
Suppliesnone$7,000
(Supplies used during December)
  • ▲increases Supplies expense $7,000
  • ▼decreases Supplies $7,000, leaving $11,000 on hand

Supplies available − supplies still on hand = supplies used

$18,000 − $11,000 = $7,000 used in December

c

Adjustment c: Equipment depreciates

Dec 31

Prepaid expense

The $140,000 of rafts, kayaks, and safety gear bought on Dec 2 will be used for years, not months. Nantahala expects to use it for five years (60 months) and then sell it for about $20,000.

Adjusting entry, December 31
AccountDebitCredit
Depreciation expense$2,000none
Accumulated depreciationnone$2,000
(One month of equipment use)
  • ▲increases Depreciation expense $2,000
  • ▼decreases Equipment, net, $2,000 (through the contra account)

(Cost − expected resale value) ÷ months of use = monthly depreciation

($140,000 − $20,000) ÷ 60 months = $2,000 per month

Depreciation spreads the cost of a long-lived asset over the periods that benefit from it. Notice the credit didn’t go to Equipment. It went to Accumulated depreciation, a contra account: an account whose normal balance is the opposite of the account it’s paired with. Equipment keeps its normal debit balance at original cost, and accumulated depreciation carries a credit balance that builds up month after month.

Reporting equipment at book value
Equipment, at original cost$140,000
Less: Accumulated depreciation(2,000)minus $2,000
Equipment, net (book value)$138,000

Cost minus accumulated depreciation is the asset’s book value, also called its carrying value. Depreciation is an estimate for spreading cost, not a measure of what the gear would sell for today, so book value and market value are usually different. Depreciation gets a deeper treatment later in the course.

Adjustment d: Advance-booked trips are delivered

Dec 31

Deferred revenue

On Dec 20, a scout troop paid $9,000 up front for a season of trips, and Nantahala recorded a liability. By Dec 31, $3,000 of those trips have been guided.

Adjusting entry, December 31
AccountDebitCredit
Deferred revenue$3,000none
Service revenuenone$3,000
(Trips delivered to customers who paid in advance)
  • ▼decreases Deferred revenue $3,000, leaving $6,000 still owed in trips
  • ▲increases Service revenue $3,000, so net income and equity go up

Deferred revenue is sometimes called unearned revenue. You’ll see it wherever customers pay first: ski season passes, gym memberships, concert tickets, and gift cards. Each time the company delivers what it owes, the liability shrinks and revenue grows.

Adjustment g: Interest builds on the bank loan

Dec 31

Accrued expense

The $120,000 note from Dec 1 charges 6% annual interest, paid once a year. Interest isn’t recorded daily, but one month of it has built up by Dec 31.

Adjusting entry, December 31
AccountDebitCredit
Interest expense$600none
Interest payablenone$600
(Interest owed for December, not yet paid)
  • ▲increases Interest expense $600
  • ▲increases Interest payable $600

Amount borrowed × annual rate × fraction of the year = interest

$120,000 × 6% × 1/12 = $600 for December

For two months, use 2/12; for three months, 3/12; and so on.

Common mistake

Don’t credit Notes payable for interest. The note tracks the amount borrowed (the principal). Interest owed goes in its own account, Interest payable, so the two are never mixed together.

The flip side

One company’s accrued expense is often another company’s accrued revenue. Fontana Lake Savings Bank, which lent Nantahala the $120,000, has earned $600 of interest by Dec 31 but hasn’t collected it. The bank’s adjustment mirrors Nantahala’s:

When no adjustment is needed

Not every transaction leads to an adjustment. Two kinds never do:

  • Transactions with owners. Issuing common stock and paying dividends don’t involve revenue or expense, so there’s nothing to update.
  • Transactions where cash and the revenue or expense happen together. When Nantahala collected cash at the time it guided trips, or paid December wages in December, everything was recorded at once. No timing gap, no adjustment.

Key point

Adjustments are needed when cash (or an obligation) comes before the revenue or expense (prepayments) or after it (accruals). They aren’t needed for owner transactions or for transactions where cash moves at the same time the revenue or expense is recorded.

Common mistake

Cash never shows up in a normal adjusting entry. Cash was either already recorded during the period or won’t move until next period. Instead, every adjustment pairs one income statement account (a revenue or expense) with one balance sheet account (an asset or liability).
