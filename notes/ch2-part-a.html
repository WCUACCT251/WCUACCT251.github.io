Part A: Measuring Business Activities

The Accounting Cycle During the Period — analyzing transactions and their effect on the accounting equation

LO 2-1  Transaction effects on A, L, SE LO 2-2  Revenues, expenses & dividends

Why We Measure Business Activities

Financial accounting has two jobs: measure what a business does, and communicate those measurements to people who need them to make decisions. Recording transactions is the measuring half; preparing financial statements is the communicating half. The full set of procedures that accomplishes this — from the moment a transaction happens to the moment it shows up on a financial statement — is called the accounting cycle. This chapter covers the steps that happen during the period; Chapter 3 picks up with the steps that happen at the end of the period.

Six Steps in Measuring a Transaction

  • Gather information from a source document (an invoice, a signed contract, a bank statement).
  • Analyze the transaction's effect on the accounting equation.
  • Determine whether each account is debited or credited.
  • Record the transaction in a journal.
  • Post the journal entry to the general ledger.
  • Prepare a trial balance to confirm debits equal credits.

Only external transactions — exchanges between the company and an outside party — get recorded this way. Hiring a new river guide doesn't change the company's assets, liabilities, or equity the day it happens, so it isn't recorded until the guide actually works and earns pay. That's an internal transaction (or, in this case, not a transaction at all yet).

Key Point

The accounting equation (Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders' Equity) must stay in balance after every single transaction. If one side changes, the other side changes by exactly the same amount.

The Three-Question Framework

For any transaction, ask:

  • What is one account affected? Does it increase or decrease?
  • What is a second account affected? Does it increase or decrease?
  • Do assets still equal liabilities plus stockholders' equity? They must — every time.

Concept Check 2-1

What is the effect on total assets if a company purchases land for $300,000 cash?

  1. Total assets go up by $300,000.
  2. Total assets go down by $300,000.
  3. There is zero effect on total assets.
  4. None of the above.
(c) One asset (land) goes up and another asset (cash) goes down by the same amount — zero net effect on total assets.

The Expanded Accounting Equation

Stockholders' equity breaks down into common stock (what investors put in) and retained earnings (profit the company has kept rather than paid out). Retained earnings, in turn, is driven by three things:

  • Revenues increase retained earnings.
  • Expenses decrease retained earnings.
  • Dividends decrease retained earnings.

So an increase in revenue ultimately increases stockholders' equity, while an increase in expenses or dividends ultimately decreases it — even though neither expenses nor dividends are the same thing.

Common Mistake

Don't let the word "revenue" in deferred revenue fool you — it's a liability, not a revenue account. The cash is collected, but the trips haven't happened yet, so the company still owes the service. Revenue isn't earned until the trips actually take place.

Common Mistake

Paying a dividend does not increase stockholders' equity, even though stockholders personally end up with more cash. We account for the company's resources — and the company has less cash and lower retained earnings after the dividend.

Concept Check 2-2

What effect does paying a cash dividend have on the accounting equation?

  1. Assets decrease and equity increases.
  2. Assets decrease and equity decreases.
  3. Assets decrease and liabilities increase.
  4. Assets increase and equity increases.
(b) Cash (an asset) decreases, and retained earnings (part of equity) decreases by the same amount.
