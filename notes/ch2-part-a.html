Why we measure business activities

Financial accounting has two jobs: measure what a business does, and communicate those measurements to the people who need them to make decisions. Recording transactions is the measuring half. Preparing financial statements is the communicating half.

The full set of procedures that connects the two, from the moment a transaction happens to the moment it shows up on a financial statement, is called the accounting cycle. These notes cover the steps that happen during the period. The steps that happen at the end of the period come later in the course.

Six steps in measuring a transaction

  1. Gather information from a source document, such as an invoice, a signed contract, or a bank statement.
  2. Analyze the transaction’s effect on the accounting equation.
  3. Determine whether each account is debited or credited.
  4. Record the transaction in a journal.
  5. Post the journal entry to the general ledger.
  6. Prepare a trial balance to confirm debits equal credits.

External transactions get recorded

An exchange between the company and an outside party, such as a customer, a bank, or a supplier, changes the accounting equation, so it is recorded.

Other events wait

Hiring a new river guide changes no assets, liabilities, or equity that day. Nothing is recorded until the guide works and earns pay. Activity that stays inside the company is not an external transaction either.

Key point

The accounting equation (Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity) must stay in balance after every single transaction. If one side changes, the other side changes by the same amount, or the change is offset on the same side, like swapping cash for equipment.

Three questions to ask every time

  1. What is one account affected? Does it increase or decrease?
  2. What is a second account affected? Does it increase or decrease?
  3. Do assets still equal liabilities plus stockholders’ equity? They must, every time.

Common mistake

Trading one asset for another, like cash for equipment, never changes total assets. It’s tempting to think a cash purchase “shrinks” the company, but one asset account goes up while another goes down by the same amount.

The expanded accounting equation

Stockholders’ equity breaks down into common stock (what investors put in) and retained earnings (profit the company has kept rather than paid out). Three things drive retained earnings.

Stockholders’ equity= Common stock+ Retained earnings

Retained earnings changes with:

  • Revenues▲ Increase retained earnings.
  • Expenses▼ Decrease retained earnings.
  • Dividends▼ Decrease retained earnings.

So revenue ultimately increases stockholders’ equity, while expenses and dividends ultimately decrease it. They are not the same thing, though: expenses are the costs of earning revenue, and dividends are profit paid out to owners.

Why does transaction 7 count as revenue even though no cash changed hands? The revenue recognition principle says revenue is recorded when the service is provided, not when cash is collected. The right to collect that $15,000 later is itself an asset: accounts receivable.

Common mistake

Don’t let the word “revenue” in deferred revenue fool you. It’s a liability, not a revenue account. The cash is collected, but the trips haven’t happened yet, so the company still owes the service. Revenue isn’t earned until the trips actually take place.

Common mistake

Paying a dividend does not increase stockholders’ equity, even though stockholders personally end up with more cash. We account for the company’s resources, and the company has less cash and lower retained earnings after the dividend.
