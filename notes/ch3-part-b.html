Preparing the financial statements

Turning the adjusted trial balance into the reports that investors, lenders, and managers actually read.

Where each account goes

Once the adjustments are posted, the hard part is done. The adjusted trial balance has every number the financial statements need. The job now is sorting each account into the right statement, in the right order.

  • Income statementRevenues and expenses. Revenues minus expenses gives net income.
  • Statement of stockholders’ equityCommon stock, beginning retained earnings, and dividends, plus net income carried over from the income statement.
  • Balance sheetAssets, liabilities, and the ending stockholders’ equity balances carried over from the statement of stockholders’ equity.

Order matters

  1. Income statement first. It produces net income.
  2. Statement of stockholders’ equity next. It needs net income to update retained earnings.
  3. Balance sheet last. It needs the ending retained earnings balance to make the equation balance.

We’ll build all three for Nantahala Paddling Academy using the adjusted trial balance from Part A.

The income statement

The income statement answers one question: did the company make a profit during the period? It lists revenues, subtracts expenses, and lands on net income.

Four things to notice

  • Revenues are the goods and services delivered to customers during the period, whether or not cash has been collected. Nantahala’s $75,000 includes the $8,000 added by adjustments (d) and (h).
  • Expenses are the costs used up to run the business during the period, whether or not they’ve been paid.
  • Net income equals total revenues minus total expenses. Nantahala earned $20,400 for its owners in its first month, a rough gauge of how well management ran things.
  • The statement covers a span of time, here December 1 through December 31. Think of it as a video of the month, not a snapshot.

Why adjustments matter

Look at what the adjustments did. Before adjusting, revenue was $67,000 and expenses were $31,000, which suggests a $36,000 profit. After adjusting, net income is $20,400. Skipping the adjustments would have overstated profit by $15,600.

The statement of stockholders’ equity

This statement tracks how each equity account changed during the period: what owners invested, what the company earned, and what it paid back out.

Equity grew from $0 to $265,400 in one month. Most of that ($250,000) came from investors buying stock. The other $15,400 is retained earnings: $20,400 of profit minus the $5,000 paid out as dividends. Dividends hand company resources to owners, so they reduce retained earnings even though they aren’t an expense.

Remember why the adjusted trial balance showed retained earnings at $0. It lists the beginning balance. This statement is where the beginning balance gets updated for the period’s net income and dividends:

Beginning retained earnings + net income − dividends = ending retained earnings

$0 + $20,400 − $5,000 = $15,400

Assets: current or long-term?

Current assets

Provide a benefit within the next year. Listed in order of liquidity, meaning how quickly they turn into cash: cash first, then receivables, then prepaid items like supplies and rent.

Long-term assets

Provide a benefit for more than a year. Nantahala’s rafts and kayaks will be in use for five years, so they belong here.

Supplies and prepaid rent will never turn into cash, so why are they current? Because they’ll be used up within the year, and that counts. A few businesses with an unusually long operating cycle (the time from providing a product or service to collecting the cash) use that cycle instead of one year as the cutoff.

Long-term assets usually fall into three groups:

  • Long-term investmentsStocks or bonds of other companies held for more than a year.
  • Property, plant, and equipmentLand, buildings, equipment, and machinery used to run the business.
  • Intangible assetsLong-lived assets with no physical form, like patents, copyrights, trademarks, and franchises.

Liabilities: current or long-term?

Current liabilities are due within one year of the balance sheet date; long-term liabilities are due after that. A few of Nantahala’s deserve a closer look:

  • Deferred revenue won’t be paid back in cash. It’s settled by guiding the scout troop’s remaining trips, which will happen within the year, so it’s current.
  • Interest payable isn’t due to the bank for 11 more months. That’s still within one year, so it’s current.
  • Notes payable isn’t due for nearly four more years, so it’s long-term.

Why split them this way? Lenders like Fontana Lake Savings Bank want to know whether the company can pay what’s coming due soon. Comparing current assets with current liabilities, a measure called the current ratio, gives a quick read. Nantahala has $282,000 ÷ $34,600, or about $8.15 of current assets for every $1 of current liabilities.

Stockholders’ equity

Both equity balances come straight from the statement of stockholders’ equity: common stock of $250,000 and retained earnings of $15,400. That updated retained earnings figure is what makes the balance sheet balance.

Common mistake

Don’t pull retained earnings from the adjusted trial balance. That $0 is the beginning balance. Use the ending balance from the statement of stockholders’ equity ($15,400), or total assets won’t equal total liabilities plus equity.

Finally, the balance sheet reports balances at a single point in time, here the close of business on December 31. The day before or the day after, the numbers could look different.

Snapshot or scan?

People often say the income statement is a video and the balance sheet is a photograph. A better comparison might be a medical scan. A photo is easy for anyone to read, but a scan takes training to interpret, and it can be a little fuzzy. Balance sheet amounts involve estimates and judgment calls (like depreciation), and getting full value from them takes the kind of accounting know-how this course is building.

Quick check

The interest Nantahala has built up on its bank loan isn’t due until 11 months after the balance sheet date. Where does Interest payable appear on the balance sheet?

Key point

The income statement, statement of stockholders’ equity, and balance sheet are all prepared from the adjusted trial balance. The income statement measures profitability (revenues minus expenses). The balance sheet shows that assets equal liabilities plus stockholders’ equity.

The statement of cash flows

One statement is left: the statement of cash flows. It reports the cash that came in and went out during the period, grouped into operating, investing, and financing activities.

It’s built differently from the other three. It explains the change in the Cash balance rather than coming straight from the adjusted trial balance, and you’ll learn to prepare it in detail later in the course.
